786-O is a human kidney cancer cell line derived from clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the VHL-driven subtype of renal epithelial malignancy. Its defining genomic lesion is biallelic inactivation of VHL, typically accompanied by canonical 3p loss, which establishes the core ccRCC pseudohypoxic state; the line is classically HIF-2α-dominant and functionally deficient for HIF-1α signaling. This VHL/HIF rewiring drives constitutive hypoxia-response transcription, including VEGF- and glucose-uptake programs, with strong metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and lipid-storage phenotypes characteristic of clear-cell histology. Biologically, 786-O is a standard model for dependency on HIF-2α activity and for evaluating synthetic-lethal vulnerabilities arising from VHL loss, especially against HIF-2α antagonism and other perturbations of hypoxia-linked metabolism, redox control, and downstream mTOR-linked growth signaling.
MEL202 is a human metastatic uveal melanoma cell line derived from posterior uveal tract melanoma, and it is classically used as a model of Gαq-driven disease rather than cutaneous melanoma; its recurrently reported oncogenic background includes an activating **GNAQ hotspot mutation** (typically **Q209**), with metastatic uveal melanoma–type copy-number abnormalities such as **monosomy 3** and **8q gain**, and many reports place it in a **class 2/BAP1-altered** genomic context. Functionally, MEL202 is dominated by constitutive **PLCβ–PKC–RAF/MEK/ERK** signaling and **Hippo pathway bypass with YAP/TAZ activation**, which supports proliferation and limits the efficacy of single-agent MEK inhibition; accordingly, the line is commonly used to probe dependencies on **PKC**, **YAP/TEAD**, and upstream **Gαq signaling**, with combination strategies targeting **MAPK plus PI3K/mTOR or FAK** pathways often showing greater activity than MEK blockade alone. Where **BAP1 loss** is present in this background, chromatin regulation and **DNA-damage-response** vulnerabilities become relevant, making **ATR/EZH2/PARP-context** synthetic-lethal strategies biologically plausible, while the overall phenotype is one of strong reliance on oncogenic signaling for survival rather than BRAF/NRAS-driven MAPK addiction.
PATU8988S (PaTu-8988S) is a human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cell line established from a poorly differentiated pancreatic carcinoma and is commonly used with PaTu-8988T to model epithelial–mesenchymal state switching. It is characterized by canonical PDAC oncogenic architecture dominated by activating KRAS signaling together with disruption of the TP53 and CDKN2A/p16-RB tumor-suppressor axis, and the PaTu-8988 family is reported to exhibit substantial chromosomal instability/aneuploidy. These lesions converge on constitutive MAPK/ERK-driven proliferation, defective G1/S checkpoint control, and relative apoptosis resistance, with a plastic/invasive phenotype that is biologically relevant for EMT-associated drug tolerance. From a genetic-interaction and synthetic-lethality perspective, the most plausible liabilities are KRAS effector signaling and downstream cell-cycle checkpoint nodes; a canonical homologous-recombination-deficient or PARP-sensitive genotype is not established for PATU8988S.
22RV1 is a human prostate adenocarcinoma cell line derived from the CWR22 xenograft after androgen ablation, and is used as a canonical model of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) with persistent androgen receptor (AR) dependence despite androgen deprivation. Its most salient genomic features for synthetic-lethal and drug-response studies are complex aneuploidy, TMPRSS2-ERG gene rearrangement, and focal AR locus amplification with constitutive expression of AR splice variants, especially AR-V7, which drive ligand-independent transcriptional activity. Functionally, 22RV1 is characterized by strong AR signaling output that is relatively refractory to ligand-binding-domain–targeted antiandrogens such as enzalutamide and abiraterone, while remaining dependent on AR-associated transcriptional machinery and co-regulators. The line also shows marked reliance on DNA-damage response and replication-stress buffering pathways, making it a frequent model for vulnerabilities to ATR/CHK1, DNA-PK, PARP, BET-bromodomain, and CDK7/9 inhibition, particularly in contexts where AR-V7 sustains high transcriptional burden and promotes cell-cycle progression and survival.
HS936T is a human soft-tissue sarcoma cell line derived from a pleomorphic liposarcoma, representing an adipocytic sarcoma context that is typically genomically complex and marked by widespread chromosomal instability rather than a single canonical oncogenic driver. Publicly available line annotations for HS936T indicate an aneuploid karyotype with multiple structural and numerical aberrations, consistent with defective genome maintenance and potential dependence on DNA damage response signaling. No recurrent, well-validated activating mutation or high-confidence focal amplification is as broadly established for HS936T as for more genetically defined sarcoma models; accordingly, biologically relevant vulnerabilities are more plausibly linked to loss of p53 pathway integrity, replication stress tolerance, mitotic checkpoint reliance, and non-homologous end joining/ATR-CHK1-mediated repair buffering. As a result, the cell line is most relevant for studying synthetic lethal interactions involving DNA repair defects, mitotic stress, and checkpoint inhibition, with a phenotype expected to include proliferative dependence on residual genome-stabilizing pathways and heightened susceptibility to agents that exacerbate DNA damage or disrupt cell-cycle checkpoints.
HS944T is a human melanoma cell line derived from metastatic cutaneous melanoma, with genomic features consistent with highly aneuploid melanoma and recurrent disruption of the canonical p53/RB cell-cycle checkpoint axis rather than a single pathognomonic oncogenic driver. Publicly available profiling and functional dependency data indicate marked alterations in chromosome structure and copy number, frequent loss-of-function lesions affecting cell-cycle and tumor suppressor pathways, and a dependency landscape dominated by stresses in mitotic progression, replication fidelity, and DNA damage response. Consistent with melanoma biology, HS944T is generally considered to exhibit dysregulated MAPK and PI3K-linked survival signaling, but its most actionable vulnerabilities are typically associated with impaired checkpoint control, vulnerability to ATR/CHK1/WEE1-axis perturbation, and reliance on anti-apoptotic buffering under genotoxic or mitotic stress. The line displays a proliferative, genetically unstable phenotype with pronounced sensitivity patterns that are often leveraged in synthetic-lethality studies targeting DNA repair defects, replication stress, and apoptosis regulation.
OVCAR8 is a high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma cell line derived from metastatic ovarian adenocarcinoma, and it is widely used as a genetically tractable model of chemotherapy-resistant, TP53-mutant disease. Genomically, it is characterized by TP53 loss-of-function and extensive chromosomal instability with a highly aneuploid, copy-number–altered karyotype; recurrent alterations reported in this line include dysregulation of cell-cycle control and frequent abnormalities affecting PI3K/AKT/mTOR-related growth signaling, although the precise oncogenic driver landscape is dominated more by broad copy-number changes than by a single defining activating mutation. Functionally, OVCAR8 exhibits compromised p53-dependent checkpoint signaling, reliance on intact replication-stress responses and DNA damage repair pathways, and a phenotype consistent with high proliferative stress and genome maintenance dependence. In drug-dependency studies, it is commonly used to probe vulnerabilities in ATR/CHK1/WEE1-mediated checkpoint control, homologous recombination–associated repair, and mitotic/cell-cycle regulators, and it often shows sensitivity patterns consistent with dependence on survival and stress-adaptation pathways rather than on a canonical single oncogene.
IPC298 is a human melanoma cell line derived from a metastatic melanoma lesion, and it is most often used as a model of BRAF-wild-type melanoma biology. Reported molecular features include loss-of-function alteration of **CDKN2A** with consequent disruption of the p16/RB checkpoint, together with common melanoma-associated copy-number complexity and aneuploidy; across published datasets, IPC298 has been used as a genetically unstable, genomically altered melanoma background rather than a single-pathway-addicted model. Functionally, the line is characterized by strong reliance on pro-survival and cell-cycle control circuitry, including dysregulation of the **RB–E2F axis**, and it has been used to study vulnerabilities linked to **apoptosis resistance** and **MAPK-pathway signaling context** in BRAF-wild-type disease. In synthetic-lethality and dependency studies, IPC298 is notable for phenotypes consistent with defective checkpoint control and heightened sensitivity to perturbations of cell-cycle regulators and survival pathways, making it a useful system for interrogating genetic interactions involving **CDKN2A loss**, mitotic stress, and apoptosis priming.
RPE1 (commonly hTERT-immortalized hRPE1) is a near-diploid, non-tumorigenic retinal pigment epithelial cell line from adult retina and is best regarded as a “normal-like” epithelial reference rather than a cancer-derived line; it is extensively used in synthetic-lethality and genetic-interaction studies because its baseline genome is comparatively stable and its oncogenic alterations are minimal. Canonical driver oncogene mutations, high-level gene amplifications, and complex copy-number abnormalities are generally absent, although extended culture can yield aneuploid subclones and clonal variation. A key biological feature is intact p53-mediated checkpoint signaling relative to many cancer lines, making DNA damage, replication stress, and spindle assembly checkpoint phenotypes highly interpretable; accordingly, perturbations in ATR/CHK1, PLK1, KIF11, Aurora kinases, and centrosome/ciliogenesis regulators often produce strong fitness defects. RPE1 cells are also widely used to model cell-cycle control, primary cilium biology, and chromosome segregation because they exhibit robust G1/S and G2/M checkpoint responses, low basal genomic instability, and high sensitivity to defects in mitotic progression, DNA replication, and ciliary assembly, rather than constitutive dependence on oncogenic pathways such as RTK–RAS–MAPK or PI3K–AKT signaling.
K562 is a human chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) cell line established from a female patient in blast crisis/erythroid blast transformation and is classically derived from hematopoietic myeloid lineage, making it one of the most widely used models of BCR::ABL1-positive leukemia. Its defining genetic lesion is the Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22)(q34;q11), generating constitutively active BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase, together with extensive additional karyotypic complexity typical of advanced CML, including aneuploidy and multiple structural abnormalities; the line is also reported to carry mutations affecting TP53 pathway integrity and other leukemia-relevant regulators, although BCR::ABL1 remains the dominant oncogenic driver. Functionally, K562 exhibits hyperactive ABL1/SRC-family, RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT signaling downstream of BCR::ABL1, with strong anti-apoptotic bias, elevated proliferative signaling, and altered redox/metabolic state; it is also widely used as an erythroid differentiation model because of its capacity for hemin- and other inducer-mediated globin expression. For genetic interaction and synthetic lethality studies, K562 is notable for dependency on BCR::ABL1 signaling and its downstream survival nodes, reliance on proteostasis and stress-response pathways under oncogenic kinase stress, and vulnerability to perturbations of apoptosis regulation, DNA damage response/repair buffering, and mitotic/cell-cycle control, all in the context of a highly aneuploid genome that often exposes context-specific essentialities.
GI1 is a human cancer cell line for which publicly established, lineage-resolved molecular annotation is limited; a confident tissue-of-origin, disease subtype, and driver alteration profile cannot be stated from the canonical cell line literature without risking misidentification. As a result, no high-confidence lineage-specific synthetic-lethality or drug-dependency profile can be asserted here. For rigorous use in genetic-interaction or dependency analyses, GI1 should be cross-validated against an authenticated source record (for example, Cellosaurus, DepMap, or the original depositor description) to confirm histology, oncogenic lesions, copy-number state, and pathway defects before inferring vulnerabilities.
HT29 is a human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line derived from a primary colon tumor, representing the epithelial lineage of colorectal cancer and commonly used as a model of microsatellite-stable, chromosomally unstable disease. It harbors a well-characterized activating **BRAF p.V600E** mutation, **TP53** mutation, and frequent **APC** loss/inactivation consistent with dysregulated WNT/β-catenin signaling; additional recurrent alterations reported in HT29 include copy-number changes and aneuploid karyotype typical of colorectal cancer, with broad chromosomal instability. Functionally, HT29 exhibits constitutive **MAPK pathway activation** driven by BRAF V600E, aberrant **WNT signaling** from APC deficiency, and strong **PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway** engagement in many experimental contexts, alongside altered **cell-cycle control** and attenuated apoptotic responsiveness. The line is also notable for a **differentiation-prone, mucin-producing phenotype**, with relatively high baseline resistance to several cytotoxic stresses compared with more apoptosis-sensitive colorectal models. From a dependency perspective, HT29 is especially informative for vulnerabilities linked to **MAPK/ERK signaling**, **BRAF pathway rewiring**, **EGFR-mediated feedback**, and, in some contexts, **autophagy and metabolic adaptation**, making it useful for synthetic-lethality studies involving oncogenic signaling dependence, stress response, and downstream survival circuitry.
MELJUSO is a human cutaneous melanoma cell line derived from a lymph node metastasis of malignant melanoma and is most often used as a model of BRAF–wild-type, NRAS-mutant melanoma. Its defining oncogenic lesion is activating NRAS mutation, typically NRAS Q61L, together with a highly aberrant karyotype that includes extensive aneuploidy and multiple structural chromosomal rearrangements rather than a single recurrent focal amplification. Functionally, MELJUSO shows constitutive MAPK pathway activation driven by NRAS, with parallel PI3K–AKT signaling often contributing to survival and drug tolerance; like many melanoma lines, it also exhibits strong dependence on proliferative signaling and stress-response buffering in the context of genomic instability. The line is frequently used in synthetic-lethality and drug-dependency studies because it is sensitive to perturbations of MAPK pathway nodes, mitotic control, and DNA damage response/proteostasis pathways, and it displays a robust apoptotic response to targeted pathway inhibition when survival signaling is sufficiently suppressed.
HeLa is a cervical adenocarcinoma cell line derived from an aggressive, HPV18-positive carcinoma of the uterine cervix, with extensive genomic instability and a highly aneuploid karyotype that reflects widespread structural rearrangement, chromosomal gains/losses, and ongoing copy-number heterogeneity. A defining oncogenic feature is integration of human papillomavirus 18 DNA, with constitutive expression of E6 and E7 driving functional inactivation of TP53 and RB1 pathways; accordingly, p53- and Rb-controlled cell-cycle checkpoints are profoundly disabled. HeLa also exhibits recurrent amplification and/or overexpression of loci implicated in proliferation and genome maintenance, including c-MYC–associated transcriptional programs and telomere/replication-stress adaptation, while harboring broad defects in DNA damage response fidelity and mitotic control that create vulnerability to agents targeting replication stress, checkpoint dependence, and mitotic progression. At the pathway level, HeLa commonly shows strong dependence on G1/S and G2/M checkpoint bypass, elevated proliferative signaling, impaired apoptosis competence, and reliance on compensatory DNA repair and proteostasis networks; these features underlie prominent synthetic-lethal interactions with ATR/CHK1/WEE1-axis inhibition, homologous recombination/replication-stress perturbation, and mitotic inhibitors, as well as a well-known phenotype of robust anchorage-independent growth, high transfectability, and rapid proliferation.
MEWO is a human cutaneous malignant melanoma cell line derived from a metastatic lesion, widely used as a BRAF-wild-type melanoma model. Genomically, it is characterized by recurrent melanoma-associated copy-number alterations and chromosomal instability rather than a single canonical oncogenic driver; reported alterations include loss of CDKN2A locus function and frequent dysregulation of the RB and p53 axis, with additional changes affecting receptor tyrosine kinase and MAPK-associated signaling components in some datasets. Functionally, MEWO displays defective cell-cycle checkpoint control, high proliferative capacity, and prominent dependence on mitogenic signaling and stress-adaptation pathways, making it relevant for studies of MAPK pathway targeting, cell-cycle vulnerability, and synthetic-lethal interactions involving checkpoint, apoptosis, and DNA-damage response networks. In drug-response contexts, it is commonly used to interrogate sensitivity to inhibitors of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK-related nodes, as well as to agents exploiting impaired G1/S control and genomic instability.
JURKAT is a human T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (T-ALL/T-LL) cell line derived from peripheral blood of an adolescent male and is widely used as a model of immature T-cell malignancy with strong relevance to genetic interaction and synthetic-lethality studies. Its genome is characterized by a highly rearranged TCR locus and recurrent T-ALL-associated lesions, including activating NOTCH1 mutations reported in many stocks, inactivating PTEN loss with consequent PI3K–AKT pathway hyperactivation, and additional lesions affecting cell-cycle and apoptotic control; copy-number and structural abnormalities are extensive, consistent with a genomically unstable leukemic background. Functionally, JURKAT cells show defective apoptotic signaling and are notably resistant to several death cues, in part due to impaired p53 pathway responsiveness and altered FAS/caspase-mediated death signaling, while also exhibiting dependence on survival signaling through NOTCH1 and PI3K–AKT–mTOR. They are commonly used to interrogate T-cell receptor proximal signaling, NF-κB, calcium signaling, and DNA-damage response vulnerabilities; in particular, their altered apoptosis/DNA-repair landscape makes them informative for synthetic-lethal targeting of checkpoint control, kinase signaling, and BCL-2-family–mediated survival programs.
HSC-5 is a human oral squamous cell carcinoma cell line derived from tongue/oral cavity epithelium, and it is primarily used as a model of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma with strong relevance to cell-matrix interaction, invasion, and therapeutic response studies. Reported genomic features are less extensively cataloged than for some major panel lines, but HSC-5 has been described as harboring complex karyotypic abnormalities typical of carcinomas, including aneuploidy and structural chromosomal rearrangements, rather than a single dominant canonical driver lesion; as such, its biology is often interpreted in the context of broad copy-number instability and altered cell-cycle control. Functionally, HSC-5 shows malignant phenotypes consistent with activated growth and motility programs, including strong proliferative capacity and invasive behavior, with frequent emphasis on dysregulated adhesion-related signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, and matrix-degrading/invasion-associated pathways. It is also used in studies of stress-response and drug-sensitivity phenotypes in squamous cancer, where vulnerabilities are often linked to dependence on proliferative signaling, survival pathways, and DNA damage–response buffering in the setting of chromosomal instability.
A375 is a human cutaneous malignant melanoma cell line derived from a primary skin melanoma, representing a BRAF-mutant, MAPK-addicted disease subtype. It harbors the canonical activating **BRAF p.V600E** mutation and is commonly reported to retain **TP53** wild-type status, with recurrent melanoma-associated alterations involving **CDKN2A** pathway disruption; broader cytogenetic instability and aneuploidy are characteristic of the line, consistent with advanced melanoma genomics. Functionally, A375 exhibits strong constitutive **MAPK/ERK signaling** driven by BRAF V600E, with downstream dependence on MEK/ERK for proliferation and survival, and this oncogenic state is a major determinant of drug sensitivity and resistance genetics. The line also shows substantial engagement of **PI3K/AKT** and survival signaling in context-dependent settings, while melanoma-associated phenotypes include invasive/mesenchymal plasticity and high adaptability to targeted-therapy stress. From a dependency perspective, A375 is highly sensitive to **BRAF and MEK inhibition**, exhibits vulnerabilities linked to **MAPK pathway suppression, cell-cycle checkpoint control, and stress-response pathways**, and is widely used to study synthetic lethal interactions arising from oncogene addiction, adaptive resistance, and apoptotic threshold regulation.
PK1 is a human ovarian carcinoma cell line, derived from a poorly differentiated serous adenocarcinoma of ovarian origin. It is notable for defects in homologous recombination DNA repair, with reported BRCA1 dysfunction and a genomically unstable background characterized by extensive chromosomal aberrations and aneuploidy; TP53 alteration is also common in this disease context and in PK1-related models. Consistent with this genotype, PK1 exhibits pronounced reliance on DNA damage response and replication-stress tolerance pathways, making it relevant to PARP inhibitor sensitivity and other synthetic-lethal strategies targeting homologous recombination deficiency. Additional phenotypes include high chromosomal instability and strong dependency on mitotic and checkpoint control for survival, with limited evidence for specific oncogenic kinase addiction; thus, its most actionable vulnerabilities are expected to lie in DNA repair, cell-cycle checkpoint, and apoptosis-evasion pathways.
293T is a human embryonic kidney–derived cell line transformed by stable integration of adenoviral E1A/E1B sequences and it is commonly classified as a HEK293 derivative rather than a bona fide tumor line from a defined primary cancer subtype; it therefore lacks a canonical oncogenic disease context, but it is highly genomically altered and functionally tumor-like. Its key engineered feature is expression of SV40 large T antigen, which inactivates TP53 and RB family tumor suppressor control, driving strong proliferative capacity, permissive episomal replication, and high transfection efficiency. Reported genomic abnormalities include the aneuploid/karyotypically complex background inherited from HEK293 ancestry, with broad chromosomal instability and variable copy-number alterations typical of immortalized transformed kidney-derived cells. Functionally, the line is defective in canonical p53- and Rb-mediated checkpoint signaling and exhibits altered cell-cycle control, enhanced tolerance to oncogenic and replication stress, and reliance on robust DNA replication/repair and proteostasis programs to sustain rapid growth and recombinant protein production. In dependency terms, 293T is prominently sensitive to perturbations that collapse general proliferation and survival machinery, including replication-associated stress response pathways, chaperone/proteasome homeostasis, and anti-apoptotic buffering, making it useful for synthetic-lethality and genetic-interaction studies in contexts where p53/Rb checkpoint inactivation and high basal proliferative drive are central variables.
PC9 is a human lung adenocarcinoma cell line derived from a metastatic pleural effusion of non-small cell lung cancer, classically used as an EGFR-addicted model. It harbors an activating **EGFR exon 19 deletion** and is typically **EGFR amplification–positive**, with strong dependence on EGFR–MAPK/PI3K signaling for proliferation and survival; it is therefore highly sensitive to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibition unless resistance emerges. PC9 also carries common NSCLC tumor suppressor alterations, including **TP53 loss-of-function** in many reports, and is frequently described as having relative **genomic instability** with additional copy-number alterations. Functionally, the line is characterized by marked **oncogene addiction**, high apoptotic priming upon EGFR blockade, and pronounced vulnerability to perturbations that further compromise **survival signaling, cell-cycle progression, and apoptotic threshold control**. In the context of genetic interactions and synthetic lethality, PC9 is especially informative for dependencies linked to **EGFR pathway buffering, MAPK feedback adaptation, PI3K/AKT signaling, and DNA damage response/cell-cycle checkpoint wiring**, making it a standard platform for studying drug resistance, pathway redundancy, and combination lethality in EGFR-mutant lung cancer.
A549 is a human lung adenocarcinoma cell line derived from a 58-year-old male with primary non-small cell lung cancer, and it is widely used as a model of KRAS-driven, smoking-associated pulmonary adenocarcinoma. Its dominant oncogenic lesion is an activating **KRAS p.G12S** mutation, accompanied by **wild-type EGFR** and frequent relative resistance to EGFR-targeted inhibition; it also commonly carries **CDKN2A/p16 loss or silencing**, **TP53 wild-type status**, and a **SMARCA4/BRG1-intact** background, with recurrent copy-number instability typical of epithelial cancer genomes. A549 cells are characterized by constitutive activation of **RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK** and **PI3K–AKT–mTOR** signaling, strong dependence on **MAPK pathway output**, and appreciable engagement of **stress-adaptation and antioxidant programs**. Functionally, they show robust **anchorage-independent growth**, high proliferative capacity, and invasive/epithelial plasticity features relevant to metastasis biology. In the context of genetic interactions and synthetic lethality, A549 is frequently informative for vulnerabilities linked to **KRAS dependence**, **MEK/ERK signaling**, **glutamine and redox metabolism**, **autophagy**, and **cell-cycle checkpoint control**, with additional sensitivity patterns often observed upon perturbation of **RTK feedback nodes**, **mTOR signaling**, and **DNA damage response buffering**.
